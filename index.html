    Overview: one urine sample, three biomarkers

    A paper sensor that detects three urinary biomarkers of kidney-transplant rejection from a single urine sample: CXCL9, NGAL and creatinine. Three parallel channels allow simultaneous rolling circle amplification (RCA) reactions, one per biomarker.

    Inspired by Hui et al. (2018), the sensor has two zones. Zone 1 holds reduced graphene oxide (rGO) with bound DNA aptaprimers specific to each biomarker, printed onto nitrocellulose. Zone 2 holds the RCA reagents, hemin to form the G-quadruplex/hemin DNAzyme, and trehalose and pullulan to keep the reagents stable at room temperature.

    Run the test yourself

    An interactive version of the sensor's assembly animation. Work through the test the way a patient would: open the lids, add the sample, and drag the right half along its rail to slide the paper sensor together.

      Device design

      Nitrocellulose, made by nitrating cellulose, is hydrophilic and porous (porosity 75–81%, absorption 2038 mg·g⁻¹), which suits a microfluidic paper sensor (Bordbar et al., 2021). Hydrophobic wax barriers outline the three channels that guide capillary flow of the sample and reaction products.

      Bovine serum albumin (BSA) is functionalized across the zones and bridge as a blocking reagent, minimizing background signal from non-specific binding to the aptamer on rGO (Xiao & Isaacs, 2012).

      Hui et al. (2018) folded the paper to bridge the sample into the RCA well. A slider is more durable and easier to handle than fragile folded paper, and lets the halves connect or disconnect freely for timed reagent addition, so the sample incubates long enough to free enough aptaprimer before RCA.

      Lower component: the load-bearing base that keeps the device rigid and flat so the paper does not deform. Upper component: a translating platform with one translational degree of freedom that mates precisely with the base, giving alignment and a reliable compressive seal between the two pads.

      Complementary linear guide rails give a kinematic constraint; chamfers centre the parts and stop them separating; a shallow recess blocks vertical travel. The sliding interfaces use an H7/g6 clearance fit: tight enough to limit lateral play, loose enough to avoid high friction.

      Both parts are PLA: lightweight with a high elastic modulus (typically 2.7–4.0 GPa). Its rigid chains pack into ordered crystal lattices that resist deformation, scratching and wear between the sliding parts.

      Hole H7
      Shaft g6

      The lid protects and mechanically secures the paper device, preventing contamination and evaporation; the sealed environment also improves fluid contact between components.

      It rotates about its hinge between 0° and 160°. It is polycarbonate: many medical-grade PCs are ISO 10993 certified, transparency is above 90% for clear detection, and it is biocompatible and resistant to alcohol stress-cracking. Aromatic rings give rigidity; carbonate linkages dissipate stress to prevent cracking under repeated sliding and locking.

      Tilt during incubation. To keep the sample in Zone 1 during the rGO–aptaprimer incubation and stop it running off the paper, the next version tilts the Zone 1 half. A pop-pin system on the top slider would give a range of heights, with a second paper holder on a jointed stand held at each pin level.

      Better tolerances. The 3D-printed prototype had looser tolerances and minor misalignments, and mating areas wore over time, shortening the device's life. Optimizing the fit would make sliding smoother and reduce wear.

      Microfluidic channel design

      Flow is passive: capillary action pulls liquid through the paper's cellulose microchannels with no external energy. Modelled in Autodesk Inventor and sized with two equations: Owens–Wendt for the contact angle, then Lucas–Washburn for how far the sample wicks over time. Change any value and the results update.

      γSL comes from the Girifalco–Good approximation, γSL = γS + γL − 2√(γSγL)[1 − β(γS − γL)²], with γS = 54.9 and γL = 70 mN/m and β = 0.0001247, giving 4.45 mN/m. The result: liquid in Zone 1 reaches Zone 2 within 5 minutes.

      SnapREACT smartphone app

      Judging colour by eye is subjective, especially at tiny biomarker concentrations. SnapREACT scans the whole sensor in one photo and reads NGAL, creatinine and CXCL9 together. The G4 DNAzyme reaction with H₂O₂, ABTS and TMB gives a teal-blue signal (Liu et al., 2018). Built in React Native with Expo for iOS and Android from one codebase, with local storage synced to Firebase.

        Try the calibration

        Each sensor carries four reference patches of known blue intensity.         Patient-centred interface and guided photography

        The average kidney-transplant recipient is 52 (Beerli et al., 2022), and patients over 70 are the fastest-growing group needing transplants (Mehta et al., 2023), so the interface is designed for older adults: four main tabs and no nested menus.

        The camera shows a frame overlay for sensor placement, with instructions that adapt to conditions: orientation guidance by default, and prompts to find better light when it is too dark or overexposed. After capture, "Use This Photo" and "Retake" allow a quality check; a progress screen covers the 10–15 second analysis.

        Limitations and future steps

        Manufacturing variability in nitrocellulose colour, reagent quantity or drying could make reference patches and wells non-uniform and skew calibration. Users should photograph the sensor against a contrasting background to help edge detection and focus.

        Researcher Akansha Prasad (McMaster) suggested imaging each well separately for accuracy; the team weighed this against minimal user input and kept one-photo scanning of all three wells.

        Time and resources prevented lab testing of the sensor and readout, so there is no standard curve yet linking blue intensity to biomarker levels. Future work: test whether the app separates healthy from graft-rejection levels, and use creatinine to normalize CXCL9 and NGAL for urine dilution. HSV colour space could detect concentrations below 5 nM better than RGB subtraction (Kurniastuti et al., 2022), and CNNs could improve edge detection in low-contrast images.

        Impeller design with CFD

        Mixing is one of the bioreactor's two core jobs, and the impeller decides it. Because it's 3D-printed, almost any shape was possible, but growing a culture with every candidate would take weeks. Instead we simulated three standard designs in the flask, each scaled to the same 25 mm diameter so only the shape changed.

        Decision: Rushton turbine

        It left only 14% of the liquid poorly mixed and cycled the culture every 1.5 s, about half the poorly mixed volume of the pitched-blade (28%) and marine (31%) designs. Its 2.8 mW draw is a tiny fraction of what the NEMA-17 motor delivers, and all three stayed far below the shear E. coli tolerates in stirred fermenters, so better mixing came at no practical cost.
